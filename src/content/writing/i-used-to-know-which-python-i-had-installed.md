Last night, Claude sent me downstairs to push the power button on my media server.

Okay, let me back up: my family and I wanted to watch a show, but the server wouldn't cooperate. I asked Claude to go figure it out. Two minutes later, Claude came back with a job for me: the machine wasn't responding; could I go reboot it?

I trudged downstairs, bounced the box, and let Claude know my mission was complete. It checked that everything was coming back up, babysat a couple services, and let me know we were good to watch our show.

All these years in tech and here I am again, holding down the power button.

I used to know my home network inside and out. I built the thing. I configured the virtualization, hooked up the storage, and figured out how to make my home NAS go. That was half the fun. These days, I happily hand over everything I can, right up to “push this button” and “plug in that cable.”

In January of 2026, I made a note about my machines: “What versions of Python do I have installed? No idea.”

When I'm building a local tool, I don't much care whether it's written in Python, Rust, or C#. Whatever the robot chooses is fine. I have opinions about who can see my data and what can talk to my network. I want my tools and games to run well. I want to sit down with my family and watch a show.

One of the biggest wins of turning my tech administration over to the robot is the documentation. The docs I wrote myself were pretty half-assed. Now the agent thoroughly documents its own work. The next time something breaks, a fresh session can read those notes, see how things are connected, and get on with the repair.

Recently, my robot alerted me to a couple failing hard drives. It told me which services the failures could affect and what data was at risk. We moved the data off those drives, and I asked it to suggest and source replacement hardware.

These days, I seem to have promoted myself to director of my own home infrastructure. Having seen what replacement drives cost, I have a little more sympathy for my leaders at work. My hardware requests always sounded pretty reasonable to me.
